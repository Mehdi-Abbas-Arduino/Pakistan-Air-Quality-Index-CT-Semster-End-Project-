PAKISTAN AIR QUALITY INDEX
DATA, AQI, PM2.5 AND BREAKPOINTS EXPLAINED
============================================================

Purpose of this document
------------------------
This document explains the air-quality concepts and data-processing choices
used by the Pakistan Air Quality Index project. It is intended for students
reading the dataset, the AQI calculator, and the machine-learning scripts.

IMPORTANT METHODOLOGY NOTICE
----------------------------
The current project code labels its PM2.5 breakpoint table as the "2024 US EPA"
method. However, the last two rows currently coded in
Scripts/aqi_calculator.py split the Hazardous range into AQI 301-400 and
401-500. The 2024 EPA rulemaking removed the separate 400 breakpoint and
shows PM2.5 concentrations of 225.5 µg/m3 and above as Hazardous, AQI 301+.

Therefore, the current calculator's final two rows do not match the published
2024 table. Do not describe the current implementation as a fully verified
2024 EPA implementation until the code and its tests are corrected together.
This discrepancy is deliberately documented here so it is not hidden by the
documentation.

1. WHAT IS AIR POLLUTION?
-------------------------
Air pollution occurs when gases, particles, or other substances are present
in the air at levels that can affect people or the environment.

The project records several pollutant variables, including:
- PM2.5: fine particles with aerodynamic diameter up to 2.5 micrometres.
- PM10: particles with aerodynamic diameter up to 10 micrometres.
- Carbon monoxide (CO).
- Nitrogen dioxide (NO2).
- Sulphur dioxide (SO2).
- Ozone (O3).
- Dust and other atmospheric variables.

PM2.5 is especially important because fine particles can travel deeply into
the respiratory system. The World Health Organization describes particulate
matter as a mixture that can include dust, soot, smoke, and liquid droplets.
See reference [3].

2. WHAT IS AQI?
---------------
AQI means Air Quality Index. It is a communication scale that turns pollutant
concentrations into an easier-to-understand index and health category.

The US EPA AQI uses a scale commonly displayed from 0 to 500:
- A lower value indicates cleaner air under that index.
- A higher value indicates greater pollution and a greater level of health
  concern.
- The categories communicate different levels of health concern.

The six familiar US AQI categories are:
- 0-50: Good
- 51-100: Moderate
- 101-150: Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups
- 151-200: Unhealthy
- 201-300: Very Unhealthy
- 301 and above: Hazardous

Reference [1] explains the AQI and its categories. The regulatory reporting
requirements and pollutant-specific tables are in reference [2].

IMPORTANT: AQI IS NOT A CONCENTRATION
-------------------------------------
PM2.5 concentration is a physical measurement or model estimate, usually
expressed in micrograms per cubic metre (µg/m3).

AQI is a dimensionless index calculated from a pollutant concentration using
a selected AQI standard and its breakpoint table.

For example:
- PM2.5 = 20 µg/m3 is a concentration.
- The AQI calculated from that concentration is an index value.

The number 20 by itself is not enough to identify which quantity is being
reported. Always show the unit for concentration and label AQI as an index.

3. WHAT IS PM2.5?
-----------------
PM stands for particulate matter. The number describes the approximate
aerodynamic diameter size class in micrometres (µm).

PM2.5 refers to fine particles with aerodynamic diameter of 2.5 µm or less.
PM10 refers to particles with aerodynamic diameter of 10 µm or less.
PM2.5 is a subset of PM10, not a completely separate non-overlapping group.

Examples of sources of particulate matter include combustion, traffic,
industry, dust, and some natural processes. The exact source cannot be
identified from a PM2.5 number alone. See WHO reference [3].

The project stores PM2.5 concentration in µg/m3. The variable name used in
the CSV and Python code is normally pm2_5.

4. WHAT ARE AQI BREAKPOINTS?
----------------------------
A breakpoint is a boundary in the pollutant concentration scale that
corresponds to a boundary in the AQI scale.

The breakpoint table answers two questions:
1. Which AQI interval contains this pollutant concentration?
2. Between which two AQI values should the concentration be interpolated?

For PM2.5, the US EPA table uses 24-hour concentration ranges. Each
concentration interval is paired with an AQI interval.

Published 2024 US EPA PM2.5 table:
+--------------------------------------+------------------+------------------------------+
| PM2.5 concentration (µg/m3), 24-hour | AQI interval     | Category                     |
+--------------------------------------+------------------+------------------------------+
| 0.0 - 9.0                            | 0 - 50           | Good                         |
| 9.1 - 35.4                           | 51 - 100         | Moderate                     |
| 35.5 - 55.4                          | 101 - 150        | Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups|
| 55.5 - 125.4                         | 151 - 200        | Unhealthy                    |
| 125.5 - 225.4                        | 201 - 300        | Very Unhealthy                |
| 225.5 and above                      | 301 and above    | Hazardous                    |
+--------------------------------------+------------------+------------------------------+

These are pollutant concentration breakpoints, not universal thresholds
for every AQI system. Other countries and organizations may use different
pollutants, averaging periods, breakpoint values, and category names.

The table above is from the US EPA's 2024 PM2.5 rulemaking and the AQI
breakpoint table in 40 CFR Part 58, Appendix G. See references [2] and [4].

5. HOW DOES BREAKPOINT INTERPOLATION WORK?
------------------------------------------
The measured or modelled concentration usually falls somewhere between
two concentration breakpoints. The AQI is calculated by linearly mapping
that concentration into the matching AQI interval.

Formula:

             (I_high - I_low)
AQI = --------------------------------- * (C - C_low) + I_low
             (C_high - C_low)

Where:
- C       = pollutant concentration after the required truncation.
- C_low   = lower concentration breakpoint for the interval.
- C_high  = upper concentration breakpoint for the interval.
- I_low   = lower AQI value paired with that interval.
- I_high  = upper AQI value paired with that interval.

In plain English:
1. Find the two concentration limits surrounding the PM2.5 value.
2. Find the AQI limits paired with those concentration limits.
3. Work out how far the concentration has moved through its interval.
4. Apply the same fraction to the AQI interval.
5. Round the final AQI to the nearest integer, following the EPA procedure.

Illustrative example using the Good interval:
- PM2.5 concentration C = 5.0 µg/m3.
- C_low = 0.0 and C_high = 9.0.
- I_low = 0 and I_high = 50.

AQI = ((50 - 0) / (9.0 - 0.0)) * (5.0 - 0.0) + 0
    = (50 / 9) * 5
    = 27.78
    ≈ 28

This example demonstrates the interpolation arithmetic. It is not a live
reading from a Pakistani city.

The EPA procedure also specifies how concentration values are truncated
before interpolation. For PM2.5, truncate to one decimal place, then
calculate and round the resulting index. See reference [2], Appendix G.

6. WHY DOES THE PROJECT USE THE US EPA BREAKPOINTS?
---------------------------------------------------
The project currently uses a PM2.5-to-AQI calculation based on the US EPA
breakpoint method because it provides a published, reproducible method and
a clear table for converting PM2.5 concentration to an AQI sub-index.

This is a project methodology choice. It does NOT mean:
- The US EPA operates the monitoring stations in Pakistan.
- The result is an official Pakistan government AQI.
- A PM2.5-only value is the complete multi-pollutant AQI.
- The US EPA breakpoint system is the only AQI system in the world.

Always label project results as "PM2.5-based US EPA AQI estimate" unless
the project later implements and validates the complete applicable AQI
methodology and official source data.

7. WHY DOES THE PROJECT NEED A 24-HOUR AVERAGE?
-----------------------------------------------
The PM2.5 breakpoint table used here is defined for a 24-hour average.
An individual hourly value is not automatically a 24-hour average.

The machine-learning preparation scripts group hourly records by city
and calendar day. They currently require at least 18 valid hourly PM2.5
observations for a city-day before calculating a daily mean.

That 18-hour rule is a project data-completeness rule. It allows a day with
some missing hourly observations to be retained, but it does not make the
partial-day mean identical to a complete 24-hour measurement. Such values
should be described as estimates based on available hourly coverage.

For rigorous reporting, document the valid-hour count and consider whether
the chosen completeness rule is appropriate for the dataset and intended
analysis.

8. WHAT DOES EACH IMPORTANT DATA COLUMN MEAN?
---------------------------------------------
The scripts expect these required fields:

- city:
  Name of the city associated with the observation.

- timestamp:
  Date and time of the observation. It is used to group hourly records into
  calendar days and years.

- pm2_5:
  PM2.5 concentration, expected in µg/m3. It is used to calculate the
  PM2.5-based AQI target.

Other pollutant and weather fields may be present:
- pm10: particulate matter up to 10 µm, usually µg/m3.
- carbon_monoxide: carbon monoxide concentration; check the source unit.
- nitrogen_dioxide: nitrogen dioxide concentration; check the source unit.
- sulphur_dioxide: sulphur dioxide concentration; check the source unit.
- ozone: ozone concentration; check the source unit.
- dust: modelled dust concentration or related variable; check metadata.
- temperature: air temperature; check whether degrees Celsius are used.
- humidity: relative humidity, normally percent.
- precipitation: precipitation amount; check the source's time interval and unit.
- wind_speed: wind speed; check whether km/h or m/s is used.
- wind_direction: wind direction, normally degrees.
- pressure: atmospheric pressure; check whether hPa or another unit is used.

Never assume a unit from a column name alone. Confirm units in the original
dataset documentation or API metadata before comparing or combining values.

9. HOW THE PROJECT PROCESSES HOURLY DATA
----------------------------------------
The ML scripts use this general sequence:

1. Read the CSV into a Pandas DataFrame.
2. Keep the city, timestamp, and PM2.5 columns needed for the AQI target.
3. Convert timestamps to datetime values.
4. Convert PM2.5 values to numeric values.
5. Remove invalid timestamps, missing city names, invalid concentrations,
   and duplicate city/timestamp observations.
6. Group records by city and calendar date.
7. Count valid hourly PM2.5 observations for each city-day.
8. Keep only days meeting the minimum valid-hour rule (18 by default).
9. Calculate the mean PM2.5 concentration for each retained city-day.
10. Convert each daily mean to a PM2.5-based AQI estimate.
11. For annual trends, average the daily AQI estimates within each city-year.
12. For the supervised regression experiment, aggregate available predictor
    columns by city-day and train models using a chronological split.

The annual model's yearly target is the mean of daily AQI estimates. It is
NOT the AQI calculated from the annual mean PM2.5 concentration.

10. WHAT ARE THE TWO ML SCRIPTS TRYING TO DO?
--------------------------------------------
A) aqi_regression.py
   This is a supervised estimation experiment. It attempts to estimate the
   daily PM2.5-based AQI from other available pollutant and weather features.
   PM2.5 is excluded from the input features because PM2.5 is used to create
   the AQI target. Including it would leak the answer into the inputs.

   It compares Linear Regression, degree-2 Polynomial Regression, and
   Random Forest Regression. It evaluates them on later dates that were not
   used for training. It does not forecast future years.

B) aqi_yearly_forecast.py
   This script first creates annual city-level AQI summaries, then fits a
   separate Linear Regression trend for each city. It extrapolates the
   historical annual trend into future years.

   A linear projection assumes the historical trend continues. It cannot
   know future policy changes, weather anomalies, industrial activity,
   traffic changes, or unexpected events. Longer projections are more
   uncertain.

11. IMPORTANT DATASET PROVENANCE NOTE
-------------------------------------
The repository contains:
  Dataset/pakistan_air_quality_final_clean_v2.csv

However, the CSV file and the repository documentation available during
this documentation update did not provide a verifiable citation identifying
the original publisher, download page, license, collection method, or
whether every row is station-measured or modelled.

Do not claim that the historical CSV came from Open-Meteo, IQAir, OpenAQ,
or another provider unless the team can verify that from the original
download record or dataset metadata.

Before final submission, add the following information after checking the
original source:
- Dataset title and publisher.
- Exact download URL or DOI.
- Date downloaded or accessed.
- License and attribution requirements.
- Geographic coverage and city list.
- Start and end dates.
- Temporal resolution (hourly, daily, etc.).
- Units for every pollutant and weather field.
- Whether values are measured, modelled, reanalysis, or a mixture.
- Any cleaning or transformation performed by the project team.

Open-Meteo is documented as the provider for the project's live API
integration. Its Air Quality API describes hourly variables and the CAMS
forecast/reanalysis sources. That API documentation is a source for the
live integration, not proof of the historical CSV's origin. See reference
[5].
